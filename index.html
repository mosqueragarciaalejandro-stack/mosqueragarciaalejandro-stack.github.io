About

I am an undergraduate at the University of Pennsylvania (Class of 2027) studying Mathematical Economics and Sociology, and a submatriculated M.S.E. candidate in Systems Engineering. My research is in political economy and development. I study how the threat of economic coercion reshapes international trade, how constitutional design shapes macroeconomic performance, and how the collapse of institutional trust redirects paths of social mobility in Latin America.

I work as a Research Assistant to Angel Alvarado at the Penn Initiative for the Study of Markets, and at the Behavior Change for Good Initiative at Wharton with Angela Duckworth and Katy Milkman. I am from Venezuela, where I have worked in civic organizing and electoral observation. I am applying to PhD programs in Economics for Fall 2027.

Working Papers

Rerouting Under Threat: Exposure, Credibility, and the Limits of Alliance Politics in Sanctioned States' Trade Diversion Poster version: "Against the Shadow of Coercion: Exposure, Anticipation, and the Limits of Political Alignment in Sanctioned States' Trade," Penn CURF. Supported by the Kanta Marwah College Alumni Society Undergraduate Research Grant.
Abstract When a sender threatens sanctions, where does the target's trade go? I merge the Threat and Imposition of Economic Sanctions (TIES) data with bilateral trade flows for 1970 to 2005 and estimate the response of target trade with a staggered difference-in-differences design (Callaway and Sant'Anna 2021). I subject every channel to a falsification framework built on matched placebo tests against never-treated dyads and triple-difference specifications. The conventional prediction that targets redirect trade toward their own allies does not survive falsification. The one identity-based channel that survives is redirection toward partners aligned with the sender itself, and it persists after controlling for the partner's pre-threat trade share. Sender credibility amplifies the target's response to a threat. I rationalize these patterns with a portfolio-reallocation model in which targets face convex costs of adjusting trade across partners.

Theses

Hedging the Network: State Control, Strategic Commodities, and the Limits of Diversification in Sanctions-Driven Trade M.S.E. Thesis, Systems Engineering. Advisors: Rakesh Vohra and Shresth Garg.
Abstract Do state-controlled economies reroute strategic trade differently from market-organized ones when they are threatened with sanctions? I build a product-level panel from CEPII BACI (HS6, 1995 to 2005) for strategic commodities, including energy, critical minerals, food staples, fertilizers, and defense-relevant inputs, and measure state control with V-Dem's index of state ownership of the economy. I test a state command hypothesis, under which state control lowers the cost of switching partners, against a lock-in hypothesis, under which it raises it. The thesis re-estimates the threat design for strategic and non-strategic goods across terciles of state control, measures how difficult each target is to isolate given the spare capacity of its alternative suppliers and buyers, and recovers the implicit objectives that targets reveal when they reroute, using the tractable inversion of Artuç, Chaudhuri, and McLaren.

The Phantasmagoric Hand Senior Honors Thesis, Sociology. Advisor: Pilar Gonalons-Pons.
Abstract I theorize how institutional failure displaces mobility aspirations onto embodied fields such as sport and performance. Drawing on O'Donnell, Benjamin, Marx, Freud, Bourdieu, Wacquant, Butler, and Federici, I develop a theory of the parasociological state: the institutions that grow in the shadow of a failing state and channel aspiration through the body. The baseball academy and the beauty pageant appear as paired pipelines, one organized around productive physical performance and the other around spectacular bodily display. I pair the theory with quasi-experimental evidence from a province-by-year panel of Argentine school dropout records.

Work in Progress

State Failure and Embodied Mobility: Institutional Trust and Sport Academies in Argentina and the Dominican Republic
Abstract When formal institutions lose credibility, do households shift investment away from schooling and toward embodied pathways such as football and baseball academies? I study Argentina's 2001 to 2002 collapse and the Dominican Republic's 2003 to 2004 banking crisis, combining Argentine Ministry of Education dropout records and the EPH household survey with a province-by-year political trust index built from Latinobarómetro. Because football academies recruit only boys, the design includes a gender-based falsification test. I am extending the design to further country cases that pair a national trust index with administrative records from a single embodied industry.
Presidentialism and Macroeconomic Performance: Evidence from Venezuela's 1999 Constitution
Abstract I estimate the effects of Venezuela's 1999 constitutional reform, which concentrated power in the presidency, using the synthetic control method with a donor pool of Latin American countries observed from 1975 to 2024. Outcomes include an index of presidential power, GDP per capita and its growth, inflation, unemployment, trade openness, oil rents, and government expenditure.
